Lesson 44: Surface Integrals

For a surface \(S\) given by \(\mathbf{R}(u, v)\) over a region \(D\) in the \(uv\)-plane, the surface area differential is below.

Surface Area Differential

\[ dS = \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|\,dA \]

Surface Area

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Surface Area} &= \iint_S dS \\ &= \iint_D \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|\,dA \end{aligned} \]

An Example

Find the area of the surface below.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}(u, v) = \langle u,\ v,\ 3 \rangle, \\ &\qquad 0 \le u \le 4,\quad 0 \le v \le 5 \end{aligned} \]

Step 1: Take the partial derivatives of \(\mathbf{R}(u, v) = \langle u,\ v,\ 3 \rangle\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u &= \langle 1,\ 0,\ 0 \rangle \\ \mathbf{R}_v &= \langle 0,\ 1,\ 0 \rangle \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Find the cross product.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v &= (0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1)\mathbf{i} \\ &\qquad {}+ (0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 0)\mathbf{j} \\ &\qquad {}+ (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0)\mathbf{k} \\ &= \langle 0,\ 0,\ 1 \rangle \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: Find its magnitude.

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\| &= \sqrt{0^2 + 0^2 + 1^2} \\ &= 1 \end{aligned} \]

So \(dS = 1\,dA\).

Step 4: Set up and evaluate the integral over \(0 \le u \le 4\), \(0 \le v \le 5\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \iint_S dS &= \int_0^4 \int_0^5 1\,dv\,du \\ &= \int_0^4 \Big[\, v \,\Big]_0^5\,du \\ &= \int_0^4 5\,du \\ &= \Big[\, 5u \,\Big]_0^4 \\ &= 20 \end{aligned} \]

Check: the surface is a \(4\) by \(5\) rectangle at height \(z = 3\), so its area is \(4 \cdot 5 = 20\).

The steps are the same as for surface area, but now the function is inside the integral.

Surface Integral of a Function

\[ \begin{aligned} &\iint_S f(x, y, z)\,dS \\ &\quad = \iint_D f(\mathbf{R}(u, v))\,\|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|\,dA \end{aligned} \]

An Example

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S z^2\,dS\), where \(S\) is the sphere below.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}(u, v) = \langle 2\sin v\cos u,\ 2\sin v\sin u,\ 2\cos v \rangle, \\ &\qquad 0 \le u \le 2\pi,\quad 0 \le v \le \pi \end{aligned} \]

Step 1: Take the partial derivatives of \(\mathbf{R}(u, v)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u &= \langle -2\sin v\sin u,\ 2\sin v\cos u,\ 0 \rangle \\ \mathbf{R}_v &= \langle 2\cos v\cos u,\ 2\cos v\sin u,\ -2\sin v \rangle \end{aligned} \]

Step 2: Find the cross product one component at a time.

The \(\mathbf{i}\)-component:

\[ \begin{aligned} &(2\sin v\cos u)(-2\sin v) - (0)(2\cos v\sin u) \\ &\quad = -4\sin^2 v\cos u \end{aligned} \]

The \(\mathbf{j}\)-component:

\[ \begin{aligned} &(0)(2\cos v\cos u) \\ &\qquad {}- (-2\sin v\sin u)(-2\sin v) \\ &\quad = -4\sin^2 v\sin u \end{aligned} \]

The \(\mathbf{k}\)-component:

\[ \begin{aligned} &(-2\sin v\sin u)(2\cos v\sin u) \\ &\qquad {}- (2\sin v\cos u)(2\cos v\cos u) \\ &\quad = -4\sin v\cos v\sin^2 u \\ &\qquad\quad {}- 4\sin v\cos v\cos^2 u \\ &\quad = -4\sin v\cos v\left(\sin^2 u + \cos^2 u\right) \\ &\quad = -4\sin v\cos v \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v \\ &\quad = \langle -4\sin^2 v\cos u,\ -4\sin^2 v\sin u, \\ &\qquad\quad -4\sin v\cos v \rangle \end{aligned} \]

Step 3: Find its magnitude. It is easier to find the magnitude squared first.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|^2 \\ &\quad = \left(-4\sin^2 v\cos u\right)^2 + \left(-4\sin^2 v\sin u\right)^2 \\ &\qquad\quad {}+ \left(-4\sin v\cos v\right)^2 \\ &\quad = 16\sin^4 v\cos^2 u + 16\sin^4 v\sin^2 u \\ &\qquad\quad {}+ 16\sin^2 v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 16\sin^4 v\left(\cos^2 u + \sin^2 u\right) \\ &\qquad\quad {}+ 16\sin^2 v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 16\sin^4 v + 16\sin^2 v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 16\sin^2 v\left(\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v\right) \\ &\quad = 16\sin^2 v \end{aligned} \]

Take the square root. Since \(\sin v \ge 0\) for \(0 \le v \le \pi\), the absolute value is not needed.

\[ \begin{aligned} \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\| &= \sqrt{16\sin^2 v} \\ &= 4\sin v \end{aligned} \]

Step 4: Write the function in terms of \(u\) and \(v\). On the sphere, \(z = 2\cos v\).

\[ z^2 = (2\cos v)^2 = 4\cos^2 v \]

Step 5: Set up the integral.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\iint_S z^2\,dS \\ &\quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} \left(4\cos^2 v\right)(4\sin v)\,dv\,du \\ &\quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} 16\cos^2 v\sin v\,dv\,du \end{aligned} \]

Step 6: Evaluate the inner integral with the substitution \(w = \cos v\), \(dw = -\sin v\,dv\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int 16\cos^2 v\sin v\,dv &= -\int 16w^2\,dw \\ &= -\frac{16}{3}w^3 + C \\ &= -\frac{16}{3}\cos^3 v + C \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\Big[ -\frac{16}{3}\cos^3 v \Big]_0^{\pi} \\ &\quad = -\frac{16}{3}(-1)^3 - \left( -\frac{16}{3}(1)^3 \right) \\ &\quad = \frac{16}{3} + \frac{16}{3} \\ &\quad = \frac{32}{3} \end{aligned} \]

Step 7: Evaluate the outer integral.

\[ \begin{aligned} \iint_S z^2\,dS &= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{32}{3}\,du \\ &= \Big[ \frac{32}{3}u \Big]_0^{2\pi} \\ &= \frac{64\pi}{3} \end{aligned} \]

We now know how to compute a surface integral \(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS\). The most important applications come from vector fields, where \(f\) involves a normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\). On the next two pages, we will see two of them: Stokes’ Theorem and flux. Stay tuned!
