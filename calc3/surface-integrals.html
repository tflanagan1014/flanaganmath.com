Lesson 44: Surface Integrals

For a surface \(S\) given by \(\mathbf{R}(u, v)\) over a region \(D\) in the \(uv\)-plane, the surface area differential is below.

Surface Area Differential

\[ dS = \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|\,dA \]

Surface Area

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Surface Area} &= \iint_S dS \\ &= \iint_D \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|\,dA \end{aligned} \]

An Example

Find the area of the surface below.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}(u, v) = \langle u,\ v,\ 3 \rangle, \\ &\qquad 0 \le u \le 4,\quad 0 \le v \le 5 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u &= \langle 1,\ 0,\ 0 \rangle \\ \mathbf{R}_v &= \langle 0,\ 1,\ 0 \rangle \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v &= \langle 0,\ 0,\ 1 \rangle \\ \|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\| &= 1 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \iint_S dS &= \int_0^4 \int_0^5 1\,dv\,du \\ &= 20 \end{aligned} \]

Check: the surface is a \(4\) by \(5\) rectangle at height \(z = 3\), so its area is \(20\).

The steps are the same as for surface area, but now the function is inside the integral.

Surface Integral of a Function

\[ \begin{aligned} &\iint_S f(x, y, z)\,dS \\ &\quad = \iint_D f(\mathbf{R}(u, v))\,\|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|\,dA \end{aligned} \]

An Example

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_S z^2\,dS\), where \(S\) is the sphere below.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}(u, v) = \langle 2\sin v\cos u,\ 2\sin v\sin u,\ 2\cos v \rangle, \\ &\qquad 0 \le u \le 2\pi,\quad 0 \le v \le \pi \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u &= \langle -2\sin v\sin u,\ 2\sin v\cos u,\ 0 \rangle \\ \mathbf{R}_v &= \langle 2\cos v\cos u,\ 2\cos v\sin u,\ -2\sin v \rangle \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v \\ &\quad = \langle -4\sin^2 v\cos u,\ -4\sin^2 v\sin u, \\ &\qquad\quad -4\sin v\cos v \rangle \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\|^2 \\ &\quad = 16\sin^4 v\cos^2 u + 16\sin^4 v\sin^2 u \\ &\qquad\quad {}+ 16\sin^2 v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 16\sin^4 v + 16\sin^2 v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 16\sin^2 v\left(\sin^2 v + \cos^2 v\right) \\ &\quad = 16\sin^2 v \end{aligned} \]

Since \(\sin v \ge 0\) for \(0 \le v \le \pi\), we have \(\|\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\| = 4\sin v\). On the sphere, \(z = 2\cos v\), so \(z^2 = 4\cos^2 v\).

\[ \begin{aligned} &\iint_S z^2\,dS \\ &\quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} \left(4\cos^2 v\right)(4\sin v)\,dv\,du \\ &\quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \left[ -\frac{16}{3}\cos^3 v \right]_0^{\pi} du \\ &\quad = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{32}{3}\,du \\ &\quad = \frac{64\pi}{3} \end{aligned} \]

We now know how to compute a surface integral \(\displaystyle\iint_S f\,dS\). The most important applications come from vector fields, where \(f\) involves a normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\). On the next two pages, we will see two of them: Stokes’ Theorem and flux. Stay tuned!
