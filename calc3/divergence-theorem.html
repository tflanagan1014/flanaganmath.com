The Divergence Theorem

On the previous page, we computed flux with a double integral over a surface. If the surface is closed, there is another way.

Let \(E\) be a solid region whose boundary \(S\) is a closed surface with outward orientation. Let \(\mathbf{F} = \langle P, Q, R \rangle\) be a vector field whose components have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(E\). Then:

The Divergence Theorem

\[ \iint_S \mathbf{F} \dotp \mathbf{n}\,dS = \iiint_E \nabla \dotp \mathbf{F}\,dV \]

Put simply, the flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) out through the closed surface \(S\) equals the triple integral of the divergence of \(\mathbf{F}\) over the solid \(E\) that \(S\) encloses.

An Example

Let \(\mathbf{F}(x, y, z) = \langle x, y, z \rangle\), and let \(S\) be the ellipsoid below.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}(u, v) = \langle 2\sin v\cos u,\ 3\sin v\sin u,\ \cos v \rangle, \\ &\qquad 0 \le u \le 2\pi,\quad 0 \le v \le \pi \end{aligned} \]

It encloses the solid below.

\[ E:\ \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{9} + z^2 \le 1 \]
  1. Find the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(S\) directly.
  2. Confirm your result using the Divergence Theorem.

Part (a)

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{R}_u &= \langle -2\sin v\sin u,\ 3\sin v\cos u,\ 0 \rangle \\ \mathbf{R}_v &= \langle 2\cos v\cos u,\ 3\cos v\sin u,\ -\sin v \rangle \end{aligned} \]

The cross product \(\mathbf{R}_u \times \mathbf{R}_v\) points inward here, so we use \(\mathbf{R}_v \times \mathbf{R}_u\) for the outward normal.

\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{R}_v \times \mathbf{R}_u \\ &\quad = \langle 3\sin^2 v\cos u,\ 2\sin^2 v\sin u, \\ &\qquad\quad 6\sin v\cos v \rangle \end{aligned} \]

On the surface, the vector field is below.

\[ \mathbf{F} = \langle 2\sin v\cos u,\ 3\sin v\sin u,\ \cos v \rangle \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{F} \dotp (\mathbf{R}_v \times \mathbf{R}_u) \\ &\quad = 6\sin^3 v\cos^2 u + 6\sin^3 v\sin^2 u \\ &\qquad\quad {}+ 6\sin v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 6\sin^3 v + 6\sin v\cos^2 v \\ &\quad = 6\sin v \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \dotp \mathbf{n}\,dS &= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} 6\sin v\,dv\,du \\ &= \int_0^{2\pi} 12\,du \\ &= 24\pi \end{aligned} \]

Part (b)

\[ \nabla \dotp \mathbf{F} = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 \]

The solid is an ellipsoid with semi-axes \(2\), \(3\), and \(1\), so its volume is \(\frac{4}{3}\pi \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 = 8\pi\).

\[ \iiint_E 3\,dV = 3 \cdot 8\pi = 24\pi \]

Both methods give \(24\pi\).

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals, Green’s Theorem, Stokes’ Theorem, and the Divergence Theorem are all versions of the same underlying principle:

Integrating a derivative over a region gives you information about what happens on the boundary of that region.

In each one below, the left side integrates a derivative over the region, and the right side describes what happens on the boundary.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Region: the interval \([a, b]\). Boundary: its two endpoints.

\[ \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a) \]

The Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals

Region: a curve \(C\). Boundary: its two endpoints.

\[ \int_C \nabla f \dotp d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)) \]

Green's Theorem

Region: a region \(R\) in the plane. Boundary: the closed curve \(C\).

\[ \iint_R \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \right) dA = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \dotp d\mathbf{r} \]

Stokes' Theorem

Region: a surface \(S\). Boundary: the closed curve \(C\).

\[ \iint_S (\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}) \dotp \mathbf{n}\,dS = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \dotp d\mathbf{r} \]

The Divergence Theorem

Region: a solid \(E\). Boundary: the closed surface \(S\).

\[ \iiint_E \nabla \dotp \mathbf{F}\,dV = \iint_S \mathbf{F} \dotp \mathbf{n}\,dS \]

The derivative changes from theorem to theorem, but the idea stays the same. In each case, we use the theorem when the other side is easier to compute.
