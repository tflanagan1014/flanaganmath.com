Derivatives of Vector-Valued Functions

Consider the below vector-valued function, \(\mathbf{r}(t)\).

\[ \mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\mathbf{i} + y(t)\mathbf{j} + z(t)\mathbf{k} \]

The derivative of the function is defined below, provided the limit exists.

Derivative of a Vector-Valued Function

\[ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(t + h) - \mathbf{r}(t)}{h} \]

Consider the below vector-valued function, \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), where \(x(t)\), \(y(t)\), and \(z(t)\) are differentiable.

\[ \mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\mathbf{i} + y(t)\mathbf{j} + z(t)\mathbf{k} \]

The derivative of the function is found by differentiating the component functions, as below.

Derivative by Components

\[ \mathbf{r}'(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}\mathbf{i} + \frac{dy}{dt}\mathbf{j} + \frac{dz}{dt}\mathbf{k} \]

This follows from the limit definition and properties of limits. First, write the difference quotient by components.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathbf{r}(t + h) - \mathbf{r}(t)}{h} &= \frac{x(t + h) - x(t)}{h}\,\mathbf{i} \\ &\qquad {}+ \frac{y(t + h) - y(t)}{h}\,\mathbf{j} \\ &\qquad {}+ \frac{z(t + h) - z(t)}{h}\,\mathbf{k} \end{aligned} \]

The limit of a vector-valued function is found component by component, so taking the limit as \(h \to 0\) gives the derivative of each component.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}'(t) &= \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x(t + h) - x(t)}{h} \right] \mathbf{i} \\ &\qquad {}+ \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{y(t + h) - y(t)}{h} \right] \mathbf{j} \\ &\qquad {}+ \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{z(t + h) - z(t)}{h} \right] \mathbf{k} \\ &= \frac{dx}{dt}\mathbf{i} + \frac{dy}{dt}\mathbf{j} + \frac{dz}{dt}\mathbf{k} \end{aligned} \]

We use a variety of notations for derivatives and vectors. Here is the same result in angle bracket notation. Consider the below vector-valued function, \(\vec{r}(t)\).

\[ \vec{r}(t) = \langle x(t),\ y(t),\ z(t) \rangle \]

The derivative of the function is found by differentiating the component functions, as below.

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} &= \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt},\ \frac{dz}{dt} \right\rangle \\ &= \langle x'(t),\ y'(t),\ z'(t) \rangle \end{aligned} \]

An Example

Consider the below vector-valued function. Find \(\mathbf{r}'(t)\).

\[ \mathbf{r}(t) = 3\sin t\,\mathbf{i} + 3\cos t\,\mathbf{j} + 0.5t\,\mathbf{k} \]

Differentiate each component.

\[ \mathbf{r}'(t) = 3\cos t\,\mathbf{i} - 3\sin t\,\mathbf{j} + 0.5\,\mathbf{k} \]

An Example

Consider the below vector-valued function. Find \(\mathbf{r}''(t)\).

\[ \mathbf{r}(t) = e^t\,\mathbf{i} + \sin t\,\mathbf{j} + \sqrt{t}\,\mathbf{k} \]

Differentiate each component, and then differentiate again. Here \(t > 0\), so that \(\sqrt{t}\) can be differentiated.

\[ \mathbf{r}'(t) = e^t\,\mathbf{i} + \cos t\,\mathbf{j} + \frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\,\mathbf{k} \]
\[ \mathbf{r}''(t) = e^t\,\mathbf{i} - \sin t\,\mathbf{j} - \frac{1}{4t^{3/2}}\,\mathbf{k} \]

Consider vector-valued functions \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) and \(\mathbf{s}(t)\), and the real-valued function \(f(t)\). If these functions are differentiable, then we have the following properties, often essential to proofs.

Scalar Multiplication Rule

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left[ f(t)\,\mathbf{r}(t) \right] &= \frac{df}{dt}\,\mathbf{r}(t) \\ &\qquad {}+ f(t)\,\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \end{aligned} \]

Dot Product Rule

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \mathbf{r}(t) \dotp \mathbf{s}(t) \right] &= \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \dotp \mathbf{s}(t) \\ &\qquad {}+ \mathbf{r}(t) \dotp \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt} \end{aligned} \]

Cross Product Rule

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \mathbf{r}(t) \times \mathbf{s}(t) \right] &= \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \times \mathbf{s}(t) \\ &\qquad {}+ \mathbf{r}(t) \times \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt} \end{aligned} \]

An Example

Consider the vector-valued functions \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) and \(\mathbf{s}(t)\) below.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}(t) &= \langle t^2,\ t,\ 10 \rangle \\ \mathbf{s}(t) &= \langle t,\ t,\ 3t \rangle \end{aligned} \]
  1. Calculate the dot product, and then differentiate.
  2. Differentiate, and then calculate the dot product.

Part (a)

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}(t) \dotp \mathbf{s}(t) &= (t^2)(t) + (t)(t) + (10)(3t) \\ &= t^3 + t^2 + 30t \end{aligned} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \mathbf{r}(t) \dotp \mathbf{s}(t) \right] = 3t^2 + 2t + 30 \]

Part (b)

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} &= \langle 2t,\ 1,\ 0 \rangle \\ \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt} &= \langle 1,\ 1,\ 3 \rangle \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} &\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} \dotp \mathbf{s}(t) + \mathbf{r}(t) \dotp \frac{d\mathbf{s}}{dt} \\ &\quad = \langle 2t,\ 1,\ 0 \rangle \dotp \langle t,\ t,\ 3t \rangle \\ &\qquad {}+ \langle t^2,\ t,\ 10 \rangle \dotp \langle 1,\ 1,\ 3 \rangle \\ &\quad = (2t^2 + t + 0) + (t^2 + t + 30) \\ &\quad = 3t^2 + 2t + 30 \end{aligned} \]

The result from part (a) matches the result from part (b), confirming the dot product rule for differentiation. I recommend doing the same thing for the cross product. Looking at an example is a good way to make sense of a general rule.
