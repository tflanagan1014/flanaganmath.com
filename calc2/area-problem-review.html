Lesson 1: Area Problem Review

We use \(\sum\) to indicate a sum.

Calculate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{4} k^2\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^{4} k^2 &= 0 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 \\ &= 30 \end{aligned} \]

Calculate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=2}^{6} k^3\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=2}^{6} k^3 &= 8 + 27 + 64 + 125 + 216 \\ &= 440 \end{aligned} \]

An Important Formula

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n} k^2 = \frac{2n^3 + 3n^2 + n}{6} \]

Calculate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{30} k^2\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{30} k^2 &= \frac{2(27{,}000) + 3(900) + 30}{6} \\ &= 9455 \end{aligned} \]

Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be the region under \(f(x) = x^2\) over \(0 \le x \le 4\). With \(n\) right-hand rectangles, each of width \(\Delta x = \frac{4}{n}\), the area is approximately the sum below.

\[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^{n} (k\,\Delta x)^2\,\Delta x &= \left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^3 \cdot \frac{2n^3 + 3n^2 + n}{6} \\ &= \frac{64}{3} + \frac{32}{n} + \frac{32}{3n^2} \end{aligned} \]

Two rectangles give 40, four give 30, and as \(n \to \infty\) the approximations approach the exact area, \(\frac{64}{3}\).

The Definite Integral

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k)\,\Delta x \]

Let \(A(x)\) be the area under the graph of \(f\) from 0 to \(x\). What is \(\dfrac{dA}{dx}\)? The answer is shocking.

Find \(\dfrac{dA}{dx}\) using the limit definition.

\(A(x + h) - A(x)\) is the thin sliver of area from \(x\) to \(x + h\). It is approximately a rectangle with height \(f(x)\) and width \(h\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dA}{dx} &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A(x + h) - A(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x)\,h}{h} \\ &= f(x) \end{aligned} \]

The area function is an antiderivative of \(f\)! This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a), \quad\text{where } F'(x) = f(x) \]

Use the Fundamental Theorem to find the area under \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([0, 4]\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^4 x^2\,dx &= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^4 \\ &= \frac{64}{3} \end{aligned} \]

It matches the limit of the rectangles.

Given \(f(x)\), an antiderivative is a function \(F(x)\) with \(F\,'(x) = f(x)\). We write \(\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C\).

Find \(\displaystyle\int 10x^9\,dx\).

\[ \int 10x^9\,dx = x^{10} + C \]

Find \(\displaystyle\int \left(5x^4 + 1\right)dx\).

\[ \int \left(5x^4 + 1\right)dx = x^5 + x + C \]

Find \(\displaystyle\int \left(e^x + \frac{1}{x} + 5\right)dx\).

\[ \int \left(e^x + \frac{1}{x} + 5\right)dx = e^x + \ln|x| + 5x + C \]

A definite integral has limits of integration. An indefinite integral does not.

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^5 x^2\,dx\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_1^5 x^2\,dx &= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^5 \\ &= \frac{125}{3} - \frac{1}{3} \\ &= \frac{124}{3} \end{aligned} \]

Sometimes the answer is negative! Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^9 \left(1 - x^2\right)dx\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_1^9 \left(1 - x^2\right)dx &= \left[x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_1^9 \\ &= (9 - 243) - \left(1 - \frac{1}{3}\right) \\ &= -\frac{704}{3} \end{aligned} \]

On this interval the graph is below the \(x\)-axis, so the integral is negative.

Why don’t we need \(+\,C\) for a definite integral? Use \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 2x\,dx\) to illustrate.

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_1^3 2x\,dx &= \left[x^2 + C\right]_1^3 \\ &= (9 + C) - (1 + C) \\ &= 8 \end{aligned} \]

The \(C\) always cancels.

One last thing: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int f(x)\,dx = f(x)\). Differentiation and integration are inverse operations!
