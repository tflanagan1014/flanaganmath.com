Lesson 34: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Finding area with a limit of rectangles is a lot of work. Antiderivatives give an amazing shortcut.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a), \quad\text{where } F\,'(x) = f(x) \]

Use the Fundamental Theorem to find the area under \(f(x) = x^2\) from \(x = 0\) to \(x = 4\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^4 x^2\,dx &= \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^4 \\ &= \frac{64}{3} - 0 \\ &= \frac{64}{3} \end{aligned} \]

It matches the limit of the rectangles! How could that be? That is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: Newton and Leibniz’s discovery that the tangent line problem and the area problem are connected.

Let \(A(x)\) be the area under the graph of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(x\). What is \(\dfrac{dA}{dx}\)? The answer is shocking.

Find \(\dfrac{dA}{dx}\) using the limit definition.

\(A(x + h) - A(x)\) is the thin sliver of area from \(x\) to \(x + h\). It is approximately a rectangle with height \(f(x)\) and width \(h\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dA}{dx} &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A(x + h) - A(x)}{h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x)\,h}{h} \\ &= f(x) \end{aligned} \]

The area function is an antiderivative of \(f\)! That is why antiderivatives find area.

An indefinite integral, \(\int f(x)\,dx\), is a family of functions. A definite integral, \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), is a number.

We don’t need \(+\,C\) for a definite integral, because it cancels.

\[ [F(b) + C] - [F(a) + C] = F(b) - F(a) \]

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^2 \left(x^2 + 3x + 2\right)dx\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_1^2 \left(x^2 + 3x + 2\right)dx &= \left[\frac{x^3}{3} + \frac{3x^2}{2} + 2x\right]_1^2 \\ &= \left(\frac{8}{3} + 6 + 4\right) - \left(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{3}{2} + 2\right) \\ &= \frac{38}{3} - \frac{23}{6} \\ &= \frac{53}{6} \end{aligned} \]

\[ \int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0 \]
\[ \int_b^a f(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

Sometimes the answer is negative! Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 (x - 3)\,dx\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \int_0^2 (x - 3)\,dx &= \left[\frac{x^2}{2} - 3x\right]_0^2 \\ &= (2 - 6) - 0 \\ &= -4 \end{aligned} \]

The region is below the \(x\)-axis, so the integral is negative.

Average Value of a Function

\[ f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \]

Find the average value of \(f(x) = x^2\) on \([0, 4]\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\text{avg}} &= \frac{1}{4 - 0}\int_0^4 x^2\,dx \\ &= \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{64}{3} \\ &= \frac{16}{3} \end{aligned} \]

Now You Try

Find the average value of \(f(x) = \sin x\) on \([0, \pi]\).

\[ \begin{aligned} f_{\text{avg}} &= \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sin x\,dx \\ &= \frac{1}{\pi}\left[-\cos x\right]_0^\pi \\ &= \frac{1}{\pi}(1 + 1) \\ &= \frac{2}{\pi} \end{aligned} \]

The Fundamental Theorem, Part 2

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right] = f(x) \]

Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle\int_1^x \left(t^3 + 1\right)dt\right]\).

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_1^x \left(t^3 + 1\right)dt\right] = x^3 + 1 \]

Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin\left(t^2\right)dt\right]\).

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_0^x \sin\left(t^2\right)dt\right] = \sin\left(x^2\right) \]

When the upper limit is a function of x, the chain rule multiplies by its derivative.

Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2} \cos t\,dt\right]\).

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_0^{x^2} \cos t\,dt\right] = 2x\cos\left(x^2\right) \]

Now You Try

Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\displaystyle\int_2^{x^3} e^t\,dt\right]\).

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_2^{x^3} e^t\,dt\right] = 3x^2 e^{x^3} \]
