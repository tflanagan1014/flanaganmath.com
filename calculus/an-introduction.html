Lesson 1: An Introduction

Most mathematicians and historians agree that calculus was developed independently by the Englishman Isaac Newton (1643–1727) and the German Gottfried Leibniz (1646–1716).

When we credit Newton and Leibniz with developing calculus, we mean that they were the first to understand the relationship between the solutions of two problems.

  • The tangent line problem
  • The area problem

Let’s review some essential ideas, and then discuss these two problems.

Through slope we try to understand the “steepness” of a line.

Slope

\[ m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \]

Note: The slope equation requires two points!

An Example

Find the slope of the line through the points \((0, 1)\) and \((2, 4)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} m &= \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} \\ &= \frac{4 - 1}{2 - 0} \\ &= \frac{3}{2} \end{aligned} \]

The line goes up 3 for every 2 to the right.

Please do not underestimate the significance of slope. Through it we measure change, and some people call calculus the study of change.

In the example above we measured how \(y\) changes as \(x\) changes. We call this the rate of change in \(y\) with respect to \(x\). In another context it could measure how distance changes with respect to time, or how luminosity changes with respect to temperature, or many other rates of change.

Find the slope of the horizontal line through the points \((2, 4)\) and \((5, 4)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} m &= \frac{4 - 4}{5 - 2} \\ &= \frac{0}{3} \\ &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

The slope of a horizontal line is zero.

All points on this line have the same \(y\)-value, 4, so its equation is \(y = 4\). The equation of a horizontal line has the form \(y = \text{number}\).

Find the slope of the vertical line through the points \((3, 2)\) and \((3, 6)\).

\[ \begin{aligned} m &= \frac{6 - 2}{3 - 3} \\ &= \frac{4}{0} \end{aligned} \]

This is undefined.

The slope of a vertical line is undefined.

All points on this line have the same \(x\)-value, 3, so its equation is \(x = 3\). The equation of a vertical line has the form \(x = \text{number}\).

Consider the question below.

\[ \frac{12}{4} = \ ? \]

A division question has an equivalent multiplication question.

\[ 4 \times \ ? = 12 \]

Multiplication comes from repeated addition.

\[ 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 \]

The answer to both questions is three, because that’s how many 4’s are needed to combine to 12.

Now consider the question below.

\[ \frac{12}{0} = \ ? \]

Its equivalent multiplication question is below.

\[ 0 \times \ ? = 12 \]

No matter how many zeros we combine, they will never add to 12.

\[ 0 + 0 + 0 + \cdots + 0 \neq 12 \]

Conclusion: We cannot divide by zero. We say division by zero is undefined.

For a circle, a tangent line touches the circle exactly once. What is the slope of a tangent line? This question is at the heart of differential calculus. As you will soon see, its answer measures an instantaneous rate of change.

The graph of the equation below is a circle. Consider the tangent line to the circle at the point \((5, 1)\). Can you find its slope?

\[ (x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 13 \]

Not yet, because the slope equation requires two points, and a tangent line touches the circle at only one. In a few weeks you’ll use calculus to show the slope is \(-\tfrac{3}{2}\).

You know area formulas for familiar shapes.

Find the area of the rectangle with corners \((1, 2)\), \((5, 2)\), \((5, 7)\), and \((1, 7)\).

The width is \(5 - 1 = 4\) and the height is \(7 - 2 = 5\).

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= lw \\ &= 5 \times 4 \\ &= 20 \end{aligned} \]

Find the area of the circle with center \((-2, 1)\) that passes through the point \((-7, 1)\).

The radius is the distance from \((-2, 1)\) to \((-7, 1)\), which is \(5\).

\[ \begin{aligned} A &= \pi r^2 \\ &= \pi \times 5^2 \\ &= 25\pi \\ &\approx 78.54 \end{aligned} \]

But many regions, like the region under a curve, are not shapes whose area formulas we learn in geometry. We cannot find those areas yet. By the end of this course, integral calculus will give us a truly amazing way to do it.

For every circle, the ratio of circumference to diameter is the same, and it equals \(\pi\).

\[ \frac{C}{D} = \pi = 3.14159265358979\ldots \]

\(\pi\) cannot be written as a fraction of whole numbers. It is irrational, with an infinite, non-repeating decimal expansion. Its irrationality was first proved in the 18th century by Johann Heinrich Lambert.

Mysteriously, \(\pi\) appears all over mathematics and science, in places that seem to have nothing to do with circles.

Gravity

Isaac Newton developed a theory of gravity. In the 20th century, Albert Einstein improved it with the equation below. Notice the \(\pi\).

\[ R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}R\,g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu} \]

Statistics

The normal distribution, central to statistics and probability, is below.

\[ y = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \]

Euler’s Identity

One of the simplest, most beautiful equations relates five of our most fundamental numbers.

\[ e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0 \]

Ramanujan
