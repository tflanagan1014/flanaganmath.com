Lesson 56: Infinite Series

Think of a series as an infinite sum.

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_k + \cdots \]

The partial sum \(S_n\) is the sum of the first \(n\) terms.

\[ S_n = a_1 + a_2 + a_3 + \cdots + a_n \]

Find \(S_3\) for \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k}{(k + 1)!}\). Give the exact answer as a simplified fraction.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_3 &= -\frac{1}{2!} + \frac{1}{3!} - \frac{1}{4!} \\ &= -\frac{12}{24} + \frac{4}{24} - \frac{1}{24} \\ &= -\frac{3}{8} \end{aligned} \]

Find \(S_6\) for \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{k^2}\) as a decimal.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_6 &= 1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{25} + \frac{1}{36} \\ &\approx 1.491389 \end{aligned} \]

A series is convergent if its partial sums approach a finite number. Otherwise it is divergent: the sums grow without bound or never settle on a single value.

Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^k}\) converges.

\[ \begin{aligned} S_1 &= \tfrac{1}{2} \\ S_2 &= \tfrac{3}{4} \\ S_3 &= \tfrac{7}{8} \\ S_n &= 1 - \frac{1}{2^n} \end{aligned} \]

As \(n \to \infty\), \(S_n \to 1\). The series converges to 1.

Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} 2^k\) diverges.

The partial sums are \(2, 6, 14, 30, \ldots\), and \(S_n = 2^{n+1} - 2\) grows without bound. The series diverges.

The Divergence Test

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{If } \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0, \text{ the series diverges.} \\ &\text{If } \lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0, \text{ the test is inconclusive.} \end{aligned} \]

Use the Divergence Test on \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{k}{k + 5}\).

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{n + 5} = 1 \neq 0 \]

The series diverges.
